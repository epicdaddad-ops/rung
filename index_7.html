Day 01 / MondayUpper body

Work down the list. Rest properly between sets — the timer in the corner is there for a reason.

Warm up first · 3 minutes

  1. Arm circles, forwards then backwards, until your shoulders feel loose
  2. 10 press-ups against a wall — slow, full range
  3. A 15 second dead hang from the bar
  4. One light set of your first exercise, well short of failure
3 sets each · the last clean rep is the last rep · 90s rest

Read this before you bench anything

  • Never bench a barbell on your own. Not once. A bar you can't press comes down on your throat and you can't get it off. Spotter, or a rack with safety bars set, or dumbbells.
  • Rungs 1–4 need no barbell at all. There is no rush to get to rung 5.
  • Get an adult who lifts, or a coach at a gym, to watch your first barbell sessions. Not a video.
  • No maxing out. No "one rep to see what I can do". That's how teenagers get hurt on this lift.

All three done? Log it. Weeks in a row beats anything you did in a single session.

Day 02 / WednesdayCore

Short session — 15 minutes. Leg raises hit the lower half, crunches the upper. Slow and controlled beats fast and many, every time.

Warm up first · 2 minutes

  1. 30 seconds of cat-cow — arch and round your back on all fours
  2. 20 dead bugs, slow, opposite arm and leg
  3. A 20 second plank to wake everything up
3 sets each · go until you can't · 60s rest

Crunches will not give you a six-pack

They build the muscle underneath. Whether you can see it comes down to how much fat sits on top, which crunches don't touch — you can't choose where your body loses fat. For most 13–16 year olds, visible abs are mainly a question of how far through puberty you are and what you were born with. Train them because a strong core makes everything else better, not as a mirror project.

Core recovers fast — if you want to add this to your Monday or Friday instead, that's fine.

Day 03 / FridayLegs

Two exercises, both single-leg. Harder than they look and unforgiving on balance for the first fortnight — that's normal, and the balance itself is part of what you're training.

Warm up first · 3 minutes

  1. 20 bodyweight squats, no weight, full depth
  2. 10 slow leg swings each leg, forwards and sideways
  3. 20 two-leg calf raises
  4. One light set of split squats holding a wall
3 sets per leg · go until form goes · 90s rest

Do the weaker leg first

Start every set with whichever leg is weaker, and make the stronger leg match its reps — don't give it extra. Most people have a noticeable gap for the first couple of months, and this is how you close it rather than widen it.

Legs done. Expect to feel this on Sunday rather than Saturday.

The bits that decide whether this worksRules

You can get every exercise right and still get nowhere if you get these wrong. In order of how much they matter.

01 · Effort — every set ends at the last clean rep

Don't count to a target and stop. Keep going until you can't complete another rep with good form — then the set is over. On most of the exercises here that means going to failure, every set. There are no rep numbers in this app on purpose: a number turns into a ceiling, and you'll stop at it long before you've actually worked.

02 · It only counts as failure if the reps were clean

The set ends when your form gives out, not when your willpower does. Reps that turn into flailing, jerking, or holding your breath aren't bonus reps — they're a different and worse exercise, and they're where injuries come from. The last rep should look like the ones before it.

Safe to take all the way, every set: press-ups, crunches, leg raises, lateral raises, calf raises. Failing on these just means you stop moving.

03 · The ones you never take to failure

  • Barbell bench press. Failing means a loaded bar sitting on your chest or throat that you can't lift off — and you can't shout for help with a bar on your windpipe. End every bench set knowing you had one more in you. If you want to find your limit, you do it with someone standing over you, never alone.
  • Pull-ups, once you're tired enough to be dropping off the bar rather than lowering off it.
  • Bulgarian split squats, once your balance goes — you'll fall sideways holding weight.
  • Anything with weight over your chest or head while you're alone in the house.

04 · Training this hard has a price

Taking every set to failure costs more recovery than stopping short. That's a real trade-off: if Monday leaves you flattened for Wednesday and Friday, you've done less total work across the week — and the week's total is what builds you. Sessions getting worse instead of better is the signal to back off, not to push harder.

05 · Progressive overload — the one you can't skip

Something has to get harder over time or your body has no reason to change. The ladder is this: more reps at your current rung, then the next rung. If nothing has got harder in the last month, nothing will happen in the next one.

06 · Tempo — two seconds down

Lower under control on every rep. The lowering half of a lift builds at least as much as the lifting half, and it's the half everybody throws away. Most people don't need more weight, they need to stop dropping it.

07 · Full range every rep

Full stretch at the bottom, full squeeze at the top. Half reps build half a muscle and let you lie to yourself about which rung you're on. A full-range rung 3 beats a bouncy rung 5.

08 · Rest properly between sets

  • Bench, pull-ups, split squats: 90–120 seconds
  • Lateral raises, abs, calves: 45–60 seconds

Resting isn't slacking. Rest short and you do fewer reps, which means less work done, which means less muscle. Sit down and wait.

09 · Write it down

Reps and rung, every session, in your notes app. Memory is generous and will tell you last week was better than it was. The log is the only thing that tells you whether you've earned the next rung.

10 · Back off every fourth week

Do the same sessions but end every set well before failure, while it still feels comfortable. You'll feel like you're wasting a week — you're not, you're letting your joints and nervous system catch up, and you'll usually come back stronger. If you're ill or in exam week, miss sessions and don't try to make them up by doubling.

11 · Consistency beats intensity

Three ordinary sessions a week for six months beats one heroic session followed by a fortnight off, every time. Nobody who trains hard for three weeks and quits looks any different at the end of it.

The scheduleYour week

Three sessions, never back-to-back. Rest days are when the building actually happens — the session is only the signal.

Moving up a rung

When you get clearly more reps than the week before — no wobbling, no breath-holding — in two sessions running, move up one rung. Expect your reps to crash back down. That drop is the whole point of the ladder.

Dropping back a rung

If your form falls apart after only a handful of reps, or something hurts in a joint rather than burning in a muscle, drop back. A rung you own builds more than a rung you survive.

Every four weeks: test yourself

Three numbers, same three every time, done fresh rather than at the end of a session. This is your progress record — real, measurable, and it doesn't depend on how the light hit the mirror that morning.

    Sleep

    Teenagers are generally advised to get around 8–10 hours — worth checking current NHS guidance for your exact age rather than trusting a number in an app. Either way it does more for your results than any change you could make to this programme. Extra hour of sleep beats extra session.

    No mathsFood

    No calorie counting here, deliberately. At your age the far more common problem is eating too little to grow on — training hard, running on toast and air, wondering why nothing changes. Build meals to a shape instead of a number.

    Every meal gets…Which looks like
    Something with proteinEggs, milk, yoghurt, chicken, mince, fish, tuna, beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, cheese, peanut butter
    Something starchyRice, pasta, potatoes, bread, oats, cereal, wraps. Fuel, not the enemy — cutting it is why people feel flat in sessions
    Something that grewAny fruit or veg. Frozen counts. Tinned counts. Beans on toast is a decent meal

    A normal training day

    • Breakfast: porridge and a banana, or eggs on toast. Not skipped, especially on training days
    • Lunch: a proper sandwich or wrap with meat, cheese, egg or beans in it, plus fruit
    • After training: a pint of milk, a yoghurt, or a sandwich. Milk is cheap and does the job
    • Dinner: whatever's cooked at home, second helping if you're still hungry
    • Hungry between meals: eat. Hunger at your age is information, not weakness

    Supplements: you need none of it

    Protein powder is expensive food in a tub — milk and eggs do the same job. Pre-workout, fat burners and anything sold as a "test booster" are not for teenagers and some are genuinely risky. If you're seriously considering a supplement, that's a conversation with a parent and a GP, not with an ad. Worth knowing that supplements are lightly regulated compared with medicines, so the label isn't a guarantee of what's in the tub.

    Two things that will wreck this

    • Cutting food to "get lean first". You can't build much while under-eating. Eat properly, train, be patient.
    • Rules that get stricter over time. If food starts feeling like rules you can break, or you're anxious about eating out, skipping meals, or thinking about food most of the day — tell a parent or your GP. Common thing to run into, and very fixable early.

    The bit nobody postsStraight talk

    Some of this is well established, some you should check with a GP or a coach who can actually see you. I've flagged which is which.

    You'll get strong before you get big

    Early on, most of your improvement comes from your nervous system getting better at the movement rather than muscle actually growing. That's the mainstream position in strength and conditioning. It means your first months look like "I can do way more reps" rather than "I look different". Normal, and not wasted.

    Puberty runs the size clock, not you

    How much visible muscle you can build, and when, is largely driven by where you are in puberty — and that varies enormously between people the same age. Someone in your year who's twice your size may just be eighteen months ahead. You can't train past that and you don't need to.

    Lifting doesn't stunt your growth

    That's the myth this whole area is built on. Properly coached resistance training is generally considered safe for young people — major sports-medicine and strength bodies have published position statements saying so, though look up current NHS or NSCA wording rather than taking this summary as gospel. The real injury risk is bad technique, ego-loading, and copying adults online.

    What you're comparing yourself to isn't real

    The physiques you see online are mostly grown men, years further into puberty, deliberately lit, often dehydrated for the shot, and sometimes taking drugs they don't mention. Your Tuesday against their highlight reel is a rigged game.

    Stop and tell an adult if…

    • You get pain in a joint — knee, shoulder, elbow, lower back — rather than muscle ache
    • Soreness lasts more than about three days, or you feel dizzy or sick during a session
    • Missing a session makes you anxious, or you'd train through illness or injury to avoid missing one
    • You're eating less to change how you look

    Before you start: run this past a parent or carer, and check with your GP first if you have any health condition or you're coming back from an injury.
